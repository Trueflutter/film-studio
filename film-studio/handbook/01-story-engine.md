# 01 · The story engine

AI has made images cheap and ideas expensive. The most common failure in AI film is no longer the render; it's a beautiful piece about nothing. This chapter is a repeatable way to find, test and build an idea before a single credit is spent, for films and for ads, and a way to use Claude as a tough story editor instead of a ghostwriter.

## The short version

1. **Someone wants something now, and something is in the way.** No want, no story. No obstacle, no tension.
2. **Pressure-test before you design.** Ten questions, scored 1-5. Nothing below 3 goes into production.
3. **Stakes are specific and personal.** "Everything" is not a stake; "she misses the last time he asks" is.
4. **A turn, then a payoff that is surprising and inevitable.** Plant it early, pay it off as a picture.
5. **Ads are single-minded.** One audience, one insight, one proposition, a product truth, and the product as hero of the turn.
6. **Design for what AI does well.** Few characters, clear geography, behaviour over speeches, text composited.
7. **Claude interrogates; you decide.** Ask for hard questions and alternatives, not for your idea.
8. **Killing an idea is a skill.** Fast, cheap, on paper.

## From seed to logline

1. **Seed:** the raw spark. An image, a feeling, a situation, a product, a "what if".
2. **Three angles:** the same seed as three different films. Change the protagonist, the genre, or the point of view. The obvious version is rarely the best.
3. **Logline:** _[Protagonist] must [goal] before/despite [obstacle], or else [stakes]._
   - Weak: _"A woman in a kitchen feels sad."_
   - Strong: _"A night-shift nurse must decide whether to answer her estranged father's call before the hospital calls her back in, knowing it may be the last time he asks."_
   The strong one has a person, a want, an obstacle, a clock and a cost, and every element can be filmed.

## The pressure test

Fill `01_story/concept.md`. Score each line 1-5. Every line needs 3 or more and the average 3.5 or more before treatment.

### Film

| # | Question | What a 5 looks like |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Want:** what do they want that a camera can see? | a concrete goal with a visible action (answer the call, get the ring back, finish the race) |
| 2 | **Why:** what's underneath? | a need, wound or fear the audience recognises, never stated aloud |
| 3 | **Obstacle:** who or what actively opposes them? | a force with its own will (a person, a clock, a rule, themselves), not "circumstances" |
| 4 | **Stakes:** what do they lose if they fail? | specific, personal, felt in a single image |
| 5 | **Choice:** where's the irreversible decision? | a moment on screen where they act under pressure |
| 6 | **Change:** who are they at the end? | different, or pointedly the same, and we see it |
| 7 | **Question:** what does the audience want to know, moment to moment? | a clear dramatic question that tightens |
| 8 | **Payoff:** surprising yet inevitable? | pays off a setup; the last image answers the first |
| 9 | **Visual potential:** an image only this film has? | a picture you'd put on the poster |
| 10 | **Feasibility:** can today's models deliver the essential moments? | the hard moments are designed around (see the audit) |

### Ad

| # | Question | What a 5 looks like |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Audience:** who exactly, believing what today? | a person you could describe at a dinner party, with a current belief |
| 2 | **Insight:** the human truth they'd nod at | not a feature; a tension in their life ("I don't overspend on big things; I bleed on small ones") |
| 3 | **Proposition:** the one thing, single-minded | one sentence, one idea |
| 4 | **Product truth:** the demonstrable reason to believe | a real, showable fact that earns the proposition |
| 5 | **Hook:** stops the thumb in 2 seconds, sound off | a face, a motion, a contradiction or a question in frame one |
| 6 | **Tension:** the problem or desire, dramatised | we feel it before we're told the answer |
| 7 | **Visual idea:** one image carries the message | the idea survives with no words |
| 8 | **Payoff:** the product is the hero of the turn | could a competitor run this ad unchanged? If yes, it fails |
| 9 | **CTA:** clear, low friction, earned | one action, said or shown once, at the right moment |
| 10 | **Feasibility and compliance:** claims, likeness, disclosure, models | claims substantiated, no fake testimonials, AI label planned |

### The feasibility audit (AI-specific)

Count the risky moments in your story and give each a mitigation: faces delivering long dialogue in close-up; precise hand work; crowds or more than two people interacting; readable text, screens and logos; fast contact action; a real, specific place or product; a character recurring across many shots. Mitigations: redesign the moment, hide it (reaction, insert, sound-only), shoot it real, composite it in post, or cut it. A concept that depends on five risky moments is a concept for a bigger budget.

## Story shapes that work in 15-90 seconds

Short films don't have room for three acts of plot. They have room for **one turn**.

| Shape | Engine | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **The decision** | the whole film is the moment before and after a choice | she turns the phone over, or doesn't |
| **The reversal** | we expect one thing; the last beat flips it | the burglar is returning something |
| **The reveal** | new information reframes everything we saw | the "stranger" has been her son all along |
| **The transformation** | before → catalyst → after, the after rhyming with the before | the empty workshop, the full workshop |
| **The escalation** | one small problem compounds to absurdity (comedy) | one spilled coffee becomes a flood |
| **The rhyme** | an opening image returns changed at the end | the same doorway, empty then full |

A 60-second beat sheet: **opening image (0-5s) → the world and the want (5-15) → disruption (15-25) → escalation (25-40) → choice or turn (40-50) → final image that answers the first (50-60).** Timings flex; the order doesn't.

## Ads: structures and lessons

- **Problem → tension → product truth → payoff.** The product resolves the tension on screen.
- **Dramatised recommendation.** A person with a real problem meets someone who knows. Lessons from the studio's teardown of two long-form paid-social ads (handbook 17): the brand appears around the middle, one signature line in a tight close-up carries the whole pitch, small specific numbers ($150, four clients a week) beat adjectives, a planted line pays off as a picture, and the "send me the link" moment happens inside the scene.
- **Demo as drama.** Show the product doing the thing, with a human reaction as proof.
- **The cutdown.** Plan the 15-second version from the same shots: hook, the single strongest beat, product truth, end card.
- **Brand linkage test:** mute it and hide the logo. Could it be for anyone else? Then the idea isn't yours yet.
- **Honesty rules:** no fabricated testimonials, no results the product can't deliver, AI characters don't claim a real biography.

## Scenes

A scene is a unit of change. For each scene, answer:
- **Who wants what, right now?**
- **What's in the way?**
- **What tactic do they try?** (Every line of dialogue is an action: to charm, to accuse, to deflect.)
- **What turns?** Something must be different at the end: knowledge, power, a relationship, a decision.
- **Enter late, leave early.** Start after the obvious beginning; end before the obvious end.

If a scene doesn't turn, it's a montage, and montages need a reason (time passing, a transformation).

## Dialogue

- **Subtext.** People rarely say what they feel. _"I'm angry you forgot my birthday"_ → _"The cake's in the fridge. If you want some."_
- **Short.** Generated clips hold about 2.5 words per second; 20-23 words fill an 8-second clip. Most good lines are under 10 words.
- **Say it once.** Never have the dialogue explain what the picture shows.
- **Speakable.** Read every line aloud. Spell unusual names and acronyms phonetically for voice models ("A.I.").
- **One voice per character.** Rhythm, vocabulary, what they never say (from the bible).

## Treatment and script

- **Treatment** (`01_story/treatment.md`): one page, present tense, beat by beat, so a reader sees and feels the film. Tone, look and sound in a few lines. If a moment can't be pictured, it can't be generated.
- **Screenplay** (`01_story/script.md`, Fountain): plain-text screenplay format that exports to Final Draft, Highland and WriterSolo. Action lines describe only what a camera sees and a microphone hears, one visible action per sentence; each sentence is a candidate shot.
- **AV script** for ads: two columns, video and audio, timed.

## Using Claude as a story editor

The `story-engine` skill has four modes. You always write first.

| Mode | Ask | Claude does |
|---|---|---|
| **Interrogator** | "Interrogate my concept" | asks one hard question at a time from the pressure test; never proposes the answer unless asked |
| **Critic** | "Critique my treatment/script" | scores against the test, flags lines that state feelings, dialogue over budget, scenes without a turn |
| **Alternatives** | "Three alternatives for my weakest line" | offers three different directions, each with its cost |
| **Formatter** | "Format as Fountain" / "as an AV script" | converts without changing your words |

Keep every version (`concept_v1.md`...). The point is your judgement getting sharper, not Claude's prose getting longer. Specialised fiction tools exist (Pocket FM's Sherpa launched Sep 2026 for long serial fiction); for films and ads, a structured Claude workflow is the core tool in this course (handbook 16).

## Weak vs strong

**Weak concept:** _"A cinematic ad for a meditation app showing calm people in beautiful places."_
Scores: audience 1, insight 1, proposition 2, product truth 1, hook 1, tension 1, visual idea 2, payoff 1 (any meditation app could run it).

**Strong concept:** Audience: new parents who've stopped sleeping. Insight: _you don't need an hour of calm; you need the next three minutes._ Proposition: _three minutes that work in the middle of the chaos._ Product truth: the sessions are three minutes and work with a baby on your chest. Hook: a father at 3 a.m., baby screaming, puts in one earbud. Tension: the screaming continues through the first minute. Payoff: his breathing slows; the baby's breathing matches his. End card. Visual idea: two breaths syncing.

## Drills

1. **Three angles (15 min).** One seed, three films. Loglines for each. Pick one and say why.
2. **Kill three (15 min).** Pressure-test three of your seeds in 5 minutes each. Kill two.
3. **Subtext pass (10 min).** Rewrite every line of your script that states a feeling.
4. **Rival test (10 min).** Swap the product in your ad concept for a competitor's. If it still works, find the product truth that only yours has.
